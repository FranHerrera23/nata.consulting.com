Your Team Answered That Question Yesterday

What a question your customers keep asking can tell you about the offer, the handoff, or a decision nobody owns.

Fran Herrera · Co-founder, Strategy & Operations

Updated November 14, 2026 · 10 min read

Download article PDFDownload worksheet

An estimator calls your lighting company with your quote open on his laptop. He has your price for fixtures. The electrician is sending his number that afternoon. The estimator wants to know whether your price includes installation before he puts both figures in front of the owner.

Your rep knows the answer. The fixture package includes delivery to the site. The electrician prices installation. If the contractor wants someone there to aim and program the fixtures, that’s a separate service. The rep walks him through it and adds a note to the quote. A week later the project manager calls about the same job. She has a PDF the estimator downloaded before the note went in.

At your Monday meeting, this shows up as two calls about installation. For the contractor, it was one unanswered question that followed the job from estimating into delivery.

Customers keep asking the same question when the answer is missing, hard to find, out of date, or unclear at the moment they need it. Sometimes the answer depends on a decision someone still has to make. Trace one inquiry back to what the customer saw and what they needed to do next. That tells you whether to clarify the offer, update a job’s status, or put someone in charge of the decision.

Why do customers ask a question that’s already answered?

A customer can have the answer in front of them and still be unable to use it. Check the version they saw, the words it used, and when they received it. A sentence in an installation guide won’t help someone who has to compare two quotes before the guide is sent.

Think about what “installation” means when you buy a kitchen from IKEA. The U.S. scope page explains that the service includes fitting cabinets and some worktops, and that setting IKEA appliances in place excludes electrical, plumbing, and gas connections. It lists other trade services separately. IKEA’s installation guide then explains a different part of the decision. At a pre-installation visit after booking, an installer checks the room against the approved plan and identifies supplementary work that may require a separate quote. IKEA U.S. installation scope · IKEA U.S. installation process

Those two pages answer two different questions. “Who connects the appliances?” matters when you’re comparing prices. “Will my existing hookups work with this layout?” takes an inspection of your kitchen. That distinction is useful in a lighting company, a flooring supplier, or a specialty contractor. Which part of your answer applies to every customer who buys that service? Which part depends on the property, the equipment, or someone else’s contract?

In the lighting example, a note near the price could say, “Fixture supply and site delivery are included. Your electrician prices installation. On-site aiming and programming are available under a separate scope.” If that boundary is standard, put a plain-language version on the service page and in the brochure buyers forward. Carry the same boundary into the quote where it becomes specific to the job. Ask the commercial lead who can approve an exception and make sure an exception appears in the revision the contractor actually accepts.

Try opening the quote from the project manager’s email, without access to the sales team’s folder. If the old PDF is still what opens, the issue has reached the handoff. Send a revised quote with the change visible, ask which version the contractor is carrying, and keep the accepted version with the job. The project manager shouldn’t have to know which rep answered the first call to find out what was agreed.

If the rep and the project manager give different answers, sit them down with two recent quotes. Did the company promise different scopes, or did one person interpret the same phrase differently? If the company has never decided who owns programming when a controls supplier is involved, settle the scope before editing the website. The sentence can only be as clear as the promise the business is prepared to make.

When is a repeated question really a request for a status update?

A status question needs a current fact about the customer’s order or project. Give the team a reliable way to see what happened, what is still uncertain, and who will check again. A clearer explanation of your standard lead time can help someone plan a future order. It has limited use when their own delivery is already in doubt.

UPS’s U.S. tracking guide makes a distinction anyone who has waited for a package can use. Label Created means UPS has received information from the sender. On the Way means it has the package in its network and a scheduled delivery date. Exception indicates a problem that may change the date, with details in the tracking record. Each status tells the recipient what UPS knows at that point. UPS’s guide to tracking statuses

Now consider a flooring supplier delivering to a hotel renovation. The account manager told the superintendent to expect Tuesday. The warehouse can confirm the material was picked, but the carrier has yet to confirm collection. The superintendent has a crew booked and needs to know by noon whether to release them.

Three people in the supplier’s office may see “scheduled for Tuesday” and each believe they’re helping by repeating it. One person needs to tell the superintendent what’s confirmed and when the next decision can be made. An illustrative update might read, “Your material is ready at our warehouse. Carrier pickup is still pending. Maria will confirm by 11 a.m. Monday whether to hold Tuesday’s crew.” The time and owner should come from the team that can actually get the carrier’s answer.

There’s a useful distinction for the CEO here. If dispatch already knows the pickup slipped and nobody told the customer, make that update part of dispatch’s job. If dispatch itself finds out late, fix how the carrier confirms pickup and decide who follows up on exceptions. The customer gets the same Tuesday call either way. The company has two different problems.

Which repeated questions should stay with a person?

Keep a person involved when someone needs to interpret a project condition, make an exception, or commit the company to a consequential answer. Give customers enough information to prepare and give the employee enough authority and context to help them.

Suppose a design studio’s client asks, “Can we switch the flooring?” At concept stage, it may be a straightforward design conversation. After approval and ordering, the same question could involve a supplier’s cancellation terms, a delivery window, and a sequence the builder has already set. An effective response starts by confirming what has been ordered, then identifies who can advise on the design and who can confirm cost and schedule. A page explaining how changes work can prepare the client for that conversation. The project team still needs to make the call on this change.

The same distinction applies at an accounting firm. A client can use an engagement checklist to gather bank statements and payroll reports. If a subsidiary’s records are incomplete and someone asks whether the engagement can still start on time, the answer depends on what’s missing and what the engagement lead can responsibly commit to. The checklist can get those facts in front of the right person earlier.

An owner can use the pattern to decide where employees need clearer authority. If a project coordinator sends every change request to a principal, look at what the principal is doing. Are they granting permission, judging a design consequence, or checking whether anyone contacted the supplier? The answer tells you which part can be prepared by the coordinator and which decision belongs with the principal.

How do you find the cause inside your own business?

Start with one question, one service and a recent run of comparable cases. Read the actual calls, emails or notes alongside the pages, documents and job records the customer saw. Look for the earliest place someone could have given the customer a usable answer, and check which team controlled it.

Ask your sales lead and a person from delivery to review the same exchange. On the lighting quote, have them open the public service page, the exact quote PDF, and the file the project manager received. Read the words on those versions before discussing what people remember saying. The U.K. Government Digital Service recommends understanding the user’s whole task across the teams involved and considering published content alongside building a new service. It’s a useful discipline for a company deciding whether the issue belongs in sales material, operations, or software. GOV.UK Service Standard

Use a Question-to-Source Map to make the review concrete. Here’s how it could look for the three situations above.

What the customer asked What they needed to decide What to inspect First change to test
“Does the fixture quote include installation?” How much to carry in the bid and who owns the work. The service page, the quote PDF the customer used, and the scope sales and delivery recognize. State the agreed boundary beside the price and carry it into the version handed to delivery.
“Are we still getting the flooring Tuesday?” Whether to keep the crew booked. The last confirmed carrier event, the customer’s scheduling cutoff, and who saw each. Have dispatch communicate the confirmed state before the cutoff and name the time of the next update.
“Can we switch the flooring?” Whether a design change is still worth making. The approval date, purchase order and supplier terms, plus who can confirm any schedule impact. Give the project lead the facts and a defined route to make or escalate the decision.

One question can have more than one cause. If some customers ask about installation because the quote is vague and others ask because the site needs unusual controls work, count them separately. Clarifying the standard scope can make the second conversation easier to recognize and more valuable to have.

How can you tell whether the change helped?

Pick a denominator that matches the question. For installation scope, use the number of comparable fixture quotes sent. Count how many prompted a basic scope question before acceptance and how many required project-specific advice. Keep an eye on inquiries that went quiet and on scope disputes after signing. You want buyers to understand the agreement earlier, and you want the right people to be able to reach your team.

For example, suppose the basic scope question came up on six of 20 quotes before the edit and on two of 20 afterward. Four fewer quotes needed that clarification in the second group. Check the mix of jobs and salespeople, read the remaining messages and see what buyers who signed understood. The numbers are illustrative. They give you a way to decide whether the change deserves a longer test, especially if the two groups came from different kinds of jobs.

Three checks to take into the next sales and operations meeting.

  • Find the exact place where the customer needed the answer, using the version they actually saw.
  • Decide who owns the answer and when it can be given. A scope boundary, a live update, and professional judgment each need different support.
  • Check whether comparable customers can take the next step and still reach someone when their situation warrants it.

Go back to the estimator with two prices open on his laptop. He needs to carry the right number into the bid by the end of the day. His project manager will inherit whatever he accepts. If both of them can open the same quote and see who does the work, the next call can be about the job itself.

Frequently asked questions

When is an FAQ page the right place for an answer?

Use an FAQ when the answer is useful across several services or situations and customers have a clear route to it. Put buying conditions beside the relevant service or price as well. A contractor comparing a quote should see the scope where they’re deciding whether to sign.

When should we automate replies to common questions?

Automate retrieval when the answer is stable or the system can read the current, authoritative record. Define who maintains that information and when to send the customer to a person. Try it first on a narrow set of questions and check the replies against real cases, including exceptions.

Who should maintain the answer when several departments use it?

Give ownership to the team that can change or confirm the underlying promise. Sales may own the standard scope, while dispatch owns the current delivery status. Name who updates the page, quote template and team instructions when the answer changes.

IKEA and UPS are public examples. The lighting, flooring, design and accounting situations are illustrative. The examples describe how answers are made available and what a business could test in its own operation.
