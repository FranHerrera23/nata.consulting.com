What Your Dashboard Learns Too Late

The report shows which deadline slipped. By then, the decision that could have saved it may be weeks behind you.

Fran Herrera · Co-founder, Strategy & Operations

November 5, 2026 · 3 min read

Find leading indicators by working backward from a missed commitment to the last decision that could have protected it. Look for evidence available before that decision, assign someone to act when it appears, and check against comparable jobs to see whether the warning earns its place in the review.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

  • Start with a late outcome and reconstruct the decisions that preceded it.
  • Attach every warning to a person, an action, and a deadline.
  • Track false alarms alongside the misses.

When does a project delay become visible?

A delay often becomes visible to different people at different times. Find the first reliable evidence of risk, then identify when it reached someone who could act. The gap between those moments tells you whether the business needs better information, a clearer decision right, or a different review cadence.

Consider an illustrative lighting design studio. A fixture substitution is waiting on client approval. The specifier sees the pending decision on Tuesday. Purchasing learns about it after the supplier’s hold expires on Friday. The studio’s monthly report ultimately records a late shipment. The report is accurate, and everyone reading it has missed the chance to protect the original date.

An actionable signal might be an unresolved approval approaching the supplier’s actual release cutoff. It helps only if someone can ask for the decision, extend the hold, or raise an alternate in time.

What makes an early warning worth keeping?

A warning earns its place when it appears while meaningful choices remain, identifies a credible risk, and leads to a proportionate action. Compare a handful of on-time and late jobs. Record when the signal appeared and how much decision time it would have bought.

A flag on every open approval will bury the consequential ones. Tie the flag to a commitment the team can verify. Then ask the person who sees it first what usually happens when they raise it. Edmondson’s study of 51 teams at one manufacturer found an association between psychological safety and learning behavior. In this setting, that suggests a question for the CEO: what does it cost someone to be the first to say the project is at risk?

What goes into a weekly exception review?

Bring one live exception and record the promise at risk, the source and time of the signal, the remaining options, the decision owner, and the next deadline. At the following review, record the outcome. Over time, retire alerts that repeatedly arrive too late or produce no useful action.

FAQ

Is a leading indicator the same as a forecast?

A forecast estimates a future outcome. A leading indicator is an observable signal that can inform an earlier decision. It can be useful even without a statistical forecast.

How early should a warning appear?

Work backward from how long the response takes. A flag that arrives on the release date may already be late if the buyer needs two days to approve a substitution.

What if warnings keep getting ignored?

Review a recent ignored warning with the recipient. Check whether they had enough time, clear authority, and a credible response. The alert design is only one part of that chain.
